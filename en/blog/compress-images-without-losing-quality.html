How to Compress Images Without Noticeable Quality Loss

"Image compression" gets used as a general catch-all term, but there are actually two completely different types of compression, and understanding the difference will save you a lot of trial and error.

Why image size actually matters

Images are usually the single biggest contributor to a page's total weight — sometimes making up more than 60% of a page's overall size. That means one oversized image alone can noticeably slow down your page load, and that's not just a poor user experience — it's also an official Google ranking factor within Core Web Vitals, especially the LCP metric (Largest Contentful Paint), which is very often an image. In other words, compressing your images isn't a technical nicety — it's a fundamental step for both SEO and visitor experience.

Type one: lossless compression

This type reorganizes an image's data more efficiently without discarding any visual detail — the resulting image is pixel-for-pixel identical to the original, just in a smaller file. The PNG format uses this type of compression by its very nature, which is why there's much less room to shrink it further.

Type two: lossy compression

This type discards extremely fine visual details that are hard for the human eye to notice, in exchange for a much larger reduction in file size. This is the foundation of the JPG format, and it's why any compression tool has a "quality" slider — the lower the percentage, the more detail gets discarded and the smaller the file gets.

Rule of thumb: a quality setting between 70% and 85% gives an excellent balance between size and visual quality for most images — you rarely need 100% quality for ordinary web images.

Which one should you choose?

If the image contains text or sharp edges and fine lines (like a screenshot or a chart), lossless compression or the PNG format is usually the better fit. Ordinary photographic images — products, people, landscapes — can handle medium-to-high lossy compression with no noticeable difference to the naked eye. For the best possible result, the modern WebP format combines the strengths of both and delivers a smaller file than JPG at roughly the same quality.

Practical steps to compress your images

Always start around 80% quality, and compare the resulting image side by side with the original on the same screen. If the difference isn't noticeable, try dropping to 70% or lower for extra savings. If you start noticing artifacts around edges or a clear loss of detail, go back up to a higher quality setting.

Common mistakes when compressing images

The most common mistake is compressing the same image more than once — every lossy compression pass adds new loss on top of the last one, so after a few rounds the result is visibly poor quality even if each individual step seemed reasonable on its own. The fix: always keep the original, uncompressed file, and compress directly from it every time instead of compressing an already compressed copy. Another common mistake is applying the same compression ratio to every image without a visual review — different images tolerate different compression levels depending on their content (lots of fine detail tolerates less, flat colors tolerate more).

Frequently asked questions

Does image compression actually affect my Google ranking?

Yes, indirectly — page load speed is an official Google ranking factor within Core Web Vitals, especially the LCP metric, which is often directly affected by the largest image on the page.

What's the ideal quality percentage for compressing web images?

A range between 70% and 85% gives an excellent balance between size and visual quality for most images, and you rarely need 100% quality.

Does compressing an image multiple times damage its quality cumulatively?

Yes, specifically with lossy compression — each new compression pass on an already-compressed file adds further loss. Always compress from the original.

Is there a difference between compressing an image and resizing it?

Yes, compression reduces file size while keeping the same dimensions, while resizing changes the actual dimensions. The two are often used together for the best result.

Conclusion

There's no single "correct" quality number that fits every image — but starting at 75-80% and visually comparing the result is the fastest way to find the right balance for your own images, keeping in mind that your site's speed and search results directly benefit from every kilobyte you save.
